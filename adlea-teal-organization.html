Origins

Reinventing Organizations by Frederic Laloux

The term Teal organization was introduced by Frederic Laloux in Reinventing Organizations (2014), a study of pioneering organizations that had departed from conventional hierarchical management in favor of fundamentally different ways of working. His research spanned health care, manufacturing, energy, retail, the nonprofit sector, and education, including a school in Berlin that entrusts students with substantial responsibility for their own learning.

Laloux observed that each major shift in human consciousness throughout history has given rise to a new organizational model. He argued that a new stage of consciousness is now emerging, and with it, organizations grounded in trust, wholeness, and a living sense of purpose.

Theoretical Foundations

Ken Wilber’s Integral Theory

Laloux’s framework draws on the Integral Theory of philosopher Ken Wilber, who also wrote the foreword to Reinventing Organizations, and on the broader field of developmental psychology. Integral Theory holds that human consciousness, in individuals and in societies alike, unfolds through a sequence of increasingly complex stages. Each stage offers a wider perspective, a more inclusive sense of identity, and a greater capacity to hold complexity.

Central to Wilber’s thought is the principle that each stage transcends and includes the one before it. Later stages do not discard earlier capacities; they integrate them into a broader whole. Following Wilber, Laloux designates each stage by a color, a convention rooted in the Spiral Dynamics model that grew from the research of psychologist Clare W. Graves. The colors offer a shared language for the developmental paradigms that shape how people organize their work.

Developmental Paradigms

The Stages of Organizational Development

Laloux identifies five organizational paradigms that have emerged over the course of human history. Each represented a genuine advance in its time, and each remains present in the world today.

  • Red · Impulsive

    Organization centered on the personal power of a single leader. Its contributions were the division of labor and command authority.

  • Amber · Conformist

    Organization built on stable hierarchy, formal roles, and replicable processes, enabling long-term planning at scale. Many government agencies, religious institutions, and traditional public school systems reflect this paradigm.

  • Orange · Achievement

    Organization oriented toward measurable results, competition, and growth. Its contributions were innovation, accountability, and meritocracy.

  • Green · Pluralistic

    Organization centered on shared values, culture, and empowerment, with responsibility to all stakeholders rather than to shareholders alone.

  • Teal · Evolutionary

    Organization understood as a living system, whose contributions are self-management, wholeness, and evolutionary purpose.

Principles

The Three Breakthroughs of the Teal Paradigm

Self-Management

Teal organizations operate through networks of peer relationships rather than chains of command. Authority is distributed: decisions are made by those closest to the work, informed by the perspectives of those affected and those with relevant expertise, a practice Laloux terms the advice process. Coordination arises from trust, transparency, and shared information rather than from hierarchical control.

Wholeness

Conventional organizations often invite people to bring only a narrow, professional self to work, leaving emotion, intuition, and identity at the door. Teal organizations cultivate environments in which people may show up as whole human beings, recognizing that such integration yields more authentic relationships, deeper commitment, and greater creativity.

Evolutionary Purpose

Laloux describes the Teal organization as having a life and a sense of direction of its own. Rather than attempting to predict and control the future through rigid strategic plans, its members practice sensing and responding: attending to what the organization’s purpose calls for and adapting as circumstances evolve.

Our Practice

How ADLEA Embodies the Teal Principles

These principles are reflected in the language and structure of ADLEA’s team. Our leaders are Guides rather than managers, and Team members hold stewardship of their domains. Wellbeing is foundational to our work, consistent with ADLEA’s commitment to human-centered education. Our Founder serves as Keeper of the Vision, attending to where ADLEA’s purpose is calling us next.

ADLEA’s Teal culture operates within its legal structure as a California nonprofit public benefit corporation. The Board of Directors and corporate officers carry the fiduciary and legal responsibilities the law requires, while ADLEA’s day-to-day work is guided by the principles described above.
